Hello Walkers—

I am writing this from Tbilisi, with August still on my mind. Most of it was spent in Bali, working remotely and moving slowly across the island: Balangan in the south, then Candi Dasa in the east, Canggu, and finally Ubud, with a twenty-hour layover in Chengdu on the way back to Rome.

I arrived with a walker's habits and discovered that Bali does not want to be walked. Outside the hotel in Candi Dasa there were almost no sidewalks, and where one existed it had given up long ago. The road belonged to scooters, trucks, and dogs. For someone who uses a twenty-kilometer walk as a way of thinking, this is a strange kind of confinement.

So I stopped trying to walk the island and started looking at what it kept putting in front of me---doors and gates---those gorgeous, majestic doors and gates picked from illustrated fantasy books set in a parallel universe.

![The split gate at Lempuyang, and the queue for the same photograph.](/flaneur/016_001.jpeg)

On the drive from Candi Dasa to Canggu we stopped at the water palaces of Taman Ujung and Tirta Gangga, the temple at Lempuyang, and the old palace in Karangasem. At Lempuyang nobody goes through the famous gate. People queue under a parasol for the same photograph. Two halves of a carved tower, the volcano between them, one person alone in the gap. It is a stage more than an entrance, and I watched the line longer than I watched the view.

I came away with almost nothing except gates. The split ones that stand open like a building sliced down the middle. The roofed ones that climb into small towers, every surface carved. And behind nearly every entrance, a short wall that stops you from seeing straight through, so you have to step in and turn.

I kept photographing them for two weeks until they became an album of their own. Temple gates, palace gates, the gates of family compounds, gates to nothing in particular. Almost all of them photographed from the road, since what lay beyond was a family's courtyard or a temple's inner yard and not mine to enter. Somewhere around Ubud I understood why they held me.

Everything I disliked about Bali was on the road side of the gate. The traffic, the plastic bags along an otherwise gorgeous beach, the beach clubs of Canggu performing a version of wealth I did not recognize. The gate was not decoration. It was the whole argument of the place, carved in stone and wood. Gardens, courtyards, water, quiet. Everything I loved was on the other side.

![Sunset on the beach in Canggu.](/flaneur/016_006.jpeg)

Ubud made the same point in a gentler way. The streets there are cleaner and older, the village south of the palace can still be walked, and at Goa Gajah the garden around the cave turned out to be far more beautiful than the famous mouth of the cave itself. A threshold is a promise about what is behind it. The good ones keep it.

![Rice fields under nets on the road inland.](/flaneur/016_007.jpeg)

One afternoon we drove inland to [The Kul Kul Farm][kulkul], a place that teaches bamboo architecture and building. After a tour of the structures, Ludo and I sat down for a few hours of bamboo carpentry and each made a tray. Bamboo is soft and its grain is predictable, and I enjoyed every minute of it. The trays are coming to the studio in Rome.

It was the only thing I made in August without a keyboard. It felt very close to bookbinding: slow, quiet, and finished when it was finished.

---

Everything else I made in August is invisible.

At the start of the month I released [Refmode][refmode], a small macOS command-line tool for switching the reference modes of Apple displays from Terminal, a script, or a Stream Deck button. If you recognize some of those words, you might like it. It is on [GitHub][refmode-gh] and Homebrew.

A few weeks later I wrote a short note, [Ship the tool, not the agent][ship], about why I keep building things this way. The valuable thing to build right now is rarely another chat window wrapped around the same LLM everyone uses. It is the capability itself, exposed as a clear tool that works for a person at a keyboard and for an agent alike. The tool holds the mechanics. Whoever drives it brings the judgment.

And at the very end of the month, while packing for Tbilisi, I gave [ttvl.co][site] the sort of redesign where everything changed and nothing looks different. Every typographic detail was standardized---how dates are written, keyboard keys that now look like keycaps (yay, skeuomorphism), shell commands that render as small terminals, tables with proper frames. The [newsletter page][newsletter] finally has an archive worth browsing, with three doors for new readers: the essay, the method, and the story so far.

Bali taught me to look at gates, and I came home and put three of them on the website. I did not plan that.
