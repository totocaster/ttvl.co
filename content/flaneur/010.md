## Gear, Gear, Gear

Recently, a few friends discovered my Mercari page, leading to jokes about how I've seemingly owned every piece of photographic gear ever produced. From the outside, this revolving door of equipment might suggest endless indecision or obsession. But there was a method to this madness.

There's a common phrase in photography circles—"Gear doesn't matter." Usually, it's evoked by people who want to make the point that it actually doesn't matter. While I agree with the sentiment of "better camera equals better photographs," I do think gear, like any other tool, does matter; but not in the way most people think.

Tools, in my world, address human needs by amplifying human capabilities. They fit into two interfaces—need and human. The first part of the tool fits _the problem_ and the second fits _the operator_.

A chisel has a sharp end to fulfill a need, and a handle to fit a human. A camera has a lens and a sensor to make an image and has pretty much all of the rest—handle, shutter button, screen and menus (or not), viewfinder, strap-lugs, and many more things—to fit the operator. If you spend even the tiniest amount of time in the YouTube or forum photosphere, you'll quickly realize how much people talk about the image-making part of the tool rather than the latter.

I'm glad those YouTube channels and forums exist—it's quite easy to understand camera capabilities because of them online. However, what you can never learn from those resources is how it fits you—the operator.

Early in 2024, I decided to tackle my problem with a brute force approach by buying every camera I ever dreamed of in sequence and trying them out in the field. Fortunately, being in Japan helps as the used camera market is very healthy, and you can resell the camera for the same or a little loss.

I did exactly that and found some interesting surprises not only regarding cameras but also my composition preferences. I was _"sure"_ square format was "not for me" and "it's hard to compose." (That's what they say on forums). But I found the 6x6 format to be an extremely pleasant constraint to work with.

I also discovered most of the "best" cameras feel incredibly uncomfortable to me. And that's great, I have one less camera system to buy/dream/want on my list.

I also managed to buy a particular 35mm camera _twice_ just to come to a peace that I do not enjoy shooting my color work on _any_ 35mm camera—so all of that got scratched from the list.

Legendary Leica for the street—turns out I shoot a lot with one hand, which makes all Leica scratched from a "dream camera" list—in fact, all manual focus cameras.

This leads me to the culmination of the experiment.

## The Gear-Freeze

The Gear Freeze isn't about settling or resigning oneself to limitations. Instead, it represents clarity—_definitive choice_. After thorough exploration, after questioning assumptions and challenging biases, you commit fully to a single setup. It's a self-imposed contract stating clearly: "This is it. I found it!" The exploration period was essential, not a distraction. It was a deliberate, guilt-free search for the tool that feels right and fits a workflow.

## Commit to the Freeze

Once decided, the Gear Freeze demands full focus. Unsubscribe from gear channels, avoid forums, and ignore new releases (What is the point of watching or reading all of those? You already have everything you need, right?). Eliminate distraction. This shift—from exploring tools to mastering your craft—is challenging but necessary. By stepping away from the endless loop of gear evaluation and stepping fully into creation, the real work begins. The Gear Freeze isn't about limiting possibilities; it’s about finally unlocking them.
